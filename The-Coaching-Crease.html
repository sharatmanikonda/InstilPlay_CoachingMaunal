A Coaching Manual · U8–U19

The Coaching Crease

Everything a junior or early-career cricket coach needs to run safe, purposeful, joyful sessions — and to grow players over years, not weekends.

BCCI / NCA aligned Ages 8–19 Batting · Bowling · Fielding Living document

How to use this manual

This is written for the coach standing at the net on a Tuesday evening — the school teacher who drew the short straw, the club dad with a Level-0 badge, the ex-player learning that playing and coaching are different crafts. You don't need to read it cover to cover. Each chapter stands alone, so you can pull out the fast-bowling workload table before a match, or the fault-diagnosis grid mid-session.

Three kinds of highlighted box recur throughout. A Coach's Note gives a practical cue you can use tomorrow. A Safety box carries a rule you must not bend. A Drill box is a ready-to-run activity with a setup, a coaching focus, and a way to make it harder or easier.

The manual grows part by part. Part 1 — the foundations of how to coach — is complete below. The remaining parts are being written and will appear in the contents rail as they land.

Coach's Note · The one idea to hold

Your job is not to produce the best eleven-year-old. It is to produce a sixteen-year-old who still loves the game, still wants to train, and has the movement skills to keep improving. Almost every decision in this manual flows from that single reframe.

Chapter 1 · Part 1 FoundationsThe coach's role & philosophy

Before a single throwdown, decide what kind of coach you are going to be.

What a junior coach actually does

New coaches assume the job is to teach technique — the perfect forward defensive, the side-on action. Technique matters, and later chapters cover it in detail. But with young players your influence runs through four channels at once, and technique is only one of them.

You are, first, an environment designer: the practice you set up teaches more than anything you say. You are a relationship: for many children you are the adult, outside their family, who decides whether sport feels safe and worth their effort. You are a role model: they copy how you treat umpires, opponents and mistakes far more faithfully than they copy your grip. And only fourth are you an instructor passing on the skills of the game.

Hold those in order. A coach who nails the fourth and neglects the first three produces skilled children who quietly drift away from cricket. The single biggest reason children leave sport is that it stopped being fun — not that they weren't good enough.

Building a coaching philosophy

A coaching philosophy is not a poster on the clubhouse wall. It is the set of principles you fall back on when you have to make a hard call in real time: whether to bat your weakest player up the order in a tight game, whether to correct a technical fault mid-match, how to react when a nine-year-old bowls a wide off the last ball. Write yours down in a few plain sentences. A useful philosophy answers three questions.

  • Why do I coach? Development, winning, giving back, keeping children active — most coaches hold several of these, but their order is what defines them.
  • What do I want players to leave with? Not just skills — habits, values, a relationship with the game.
  • How will people be treated in my team? The non-negotiables of behaviour, for you and for them.
Coach's Note · Winning vs. developing

These are not opposites, but at junior level they can pull apart in a single match. The test: would this decision look right if a talent scout and the child's parent were both watching? Giving everyone a bat, bowling your spinners in a tight finish, and losing — that is often the developmental win. Save the ruthless best-eleven cricket for when the players have chosen that path themselves.

The code of conduct you model

Every board — the BCCI, the ECB, Cricket Australia — publishes a coaches' code of conduct, and they converge on the same core. Treat every player, official and opponent with respect. Never coach under the influence of alcohol. Put player welfare and safety above winning and above your own reputation. Keep appropriate boundaries with children — the safeguarding chapter makes these concrete. Use positive language; never humiliate, single out, or use physical activity as punishment. Play and uphold the Spirit of Cricket.

The Spirit of Cricket, printed in the Preamble to the Laws, is not decoration. It is the one part of the rulebook that is explicitly the captain's — and by extension the coach's — responsibility. Model it, name it out loud when you see it, and praise it in your players as readily as you praise a cover drive.

The reflective coach

You will get better the same way your players do: by paying honest attention to what happened and adjusting. After each session ask three questions — What did I intend? What actually happened for the players? What will I change next time? A one-line note per session, kept over a season, is worth more than any single course. Chapter 21 develops this into a full self-review; for now, simply start the habit.

Chapter 2 · Part 1 FoundationsHow young players learn

Coach the child in front of you, not a small adult.

A twelve-year-old is not a scaled-down international. Their bones, attention, coordination and motivation are all on their own developmental clock, and good coaching meets that clock rather than fighting it. Two frameworks make this practical: the stages of long-term development, and what actually improves at each age.

Long-Term Athlete Development (LTAD)

The LTAD model, adapted for cricket by boards worldwide, describes the journey from first contact with a bat to lifelong play as a sequence of stages defined by developmental age, not birthday. Children of the same age can be three or four years apart biologically, so treat these as overlapping phases, not hard cut-offs.

Ages ~6–9

FUNdamentals

Build general movement literacy — running, jumping, throwing, catching, balancing. Cricket as one of several sports. Everything through games; almost nothing through instruction. Keep it loud, busy and fun.

Ages ~8–12

Learn to Train

The single best window to acquire cricket's core skills. Coordination is highly trainable; children can absorb the basics of batting, bowling and fielding technique. Volume of quality repetitions matters more than competition. Do not over-format into serious league cricket yet.

Ages ~11–15

Train to Train

The growth spurt arrives. Aerobic capacity and strength become trainable; coordination may temporarily wobble as limbs lengthen. Consolidate technique, introduce tactics and roles, and manage fast-bowling load carefully (Chapter 7).

Ages ~15–18+

Train to Compete / Train to Win

Specialisation by role and format. Higher training loads, individual development plans, mental-skills work, and genuine competitive intent. Only now does chasing results become a primary aim.

Any age

Active for Life

The stage most players actually reach. A parallel goal at every level: leave every child able and willing to play recreational cricket for decades.

Coach's Note · The skill-hungry years

Ages roughly 8–12 are golden for skill acquisition. A child who learns a sound bowling action here rarely has to unlearn it later; one who is drilled only for match results often carries a rushed, injury-prone action for years. If you coach this age group, you have the most valuable job in the club — invest the reps in quality of movement, not scoreboard.

Growth, maturation and the awkward phase

During the adolescent growth spurt (peak height velocity — often around 12 for girls, 14 for boys, but hugely variable), a player's arms and legs grow faster than their nervous system recalibrates. A previously fluent fielder may suddenly look clumsy. This is normal and temporary. Respond with patience, not criticism: reduce complexity briefly, keep confidence high, and never let a child believe they have "lost it."

Two consequences follow. First, relative age effect: within a single age group, children born early in the selection year are on average bigger and are over-selected, while late-born and late-maturing children — often the ones with the best long-term potential — get overlooked. Guard against it consciously. Second, early physical maturers can dominate junior cricket through size alone, masking whether their skills are actually developing. Judge skill, not just output.

Attention and the shape of a session

Younger children have short attention spans and learn by doing, not by listening. Practical rules of thumb: keep any single explanation under about thirty seconds; keep every child active rather than queuing; change activity before boredom rather than after; and finish on a high. A rough guide to how long one activity holds attention is "age in minutes" — a group of eights will not stay with one drill much past eight to ten minutes, while sixteens can work a theme for half an hour.

Chapter 3 · Part 1 FoundationsCoaching methods that work

How you set up practice matters more than what you say during it.

For decades cricket was taught by lining children up, demonstrating the "correct" technique, and drilling it in isolation — twenty forward defensives against a coach's throwdown before anyone faced a real ball. Modern skill-acquisition research has largely overturned this. Skills learned in isolation, in blocked repetition, often look good in the net and then collapse in a match. The methods below transfer better to the middle.

Game-based coaching (Teaching Games for Sense / TGfU)

Instead of skill-then-game, start with a modified game that exaggerates the problem you want players to solve, let them struggle with it, then coach into that need. Want to teach running between wickets? Play a game where singles are worth double. Want batters to hit gaps? Reward placement, not power. The game creates the why; your coaching supplies the how at the moment the player wants it. Learning that happens inside a game — with a bowler, a decision and consequences — transfers to matches far better than closed drills.

The constraints-led approach

Rather than telling a player what to do with their body, change the task or environment so the better movement emerges naturally. This is one of the most powerful tools you have, because it lets players self-organise without paralysis-by-instruction.

  • Task constraints: shrink the target, add a scoring rule, limit the shots allowed, change the ball (a heavier ball slows a fast bowler's arm and smooths the action).
  • Environment constraints: move the pitch length, use a slope, narrow the "safe" scoring zones, play in low light to force better watching of the ball.
  • Individual constraints: a small tweak to the player themselves — a cue, a start position, a piece of equipment such as a headband target for keeping the head still.
Coach's Note · Constrain, don't command

A batter lunging at the ball? Rather than "get your head over it" ten times, place a cone just outside off stump they must lean toward, or feed the ball a fraction fuller. The body finds the solution the task demands, and the player owns it. Save direct instruction for when a constraint can't do the job.

How much to repeat, and how varied

Two research findings reshape how you structure reps. Blocked practice (the same thing over and over) produces fast improvement in the session but poor retention. Random/varied practice (mixing deliveries, shots, tasks) feels messier and slower on the day but is remembered and transferred far better. So mix your feeds: don't bowl fifteen half-volleys in a row; bowl a length, then a short one, then full. The batter learns to read and respond, which is what matches demand.

This is the difference between training and practising. Grooving one movement has its place — early in learning a new skill, or rebuilding after an injury. But most of your time should look like the game: unpredictable, decision-rich, and just hard enough that players succeed maybe six or seven times out of ten. That success rate — challenging but achievable — is the sweet spot for learning.

Discovery vs. instruction — a balance

None of this means never demonstrate or never explain. Direct instruction is efficient for safety-critical basics and for absolute beginners who have no movement to build on. The art is knowing when to step back: give a clear demonstration and a single cue, then let the game and the constraints do most of the teaching, intervening only when a player is stuck or unsafe. Talk less than feels natural. Silence, with a well-designed practice running, is often your best coaching.

Chapter 4 · Part 1 FoundationsCommunication & feedback

The right word at the right moment; far fewer words than you think.

Cueing: external beats internal

How you phrase a coaching point changes how well it works. Internal cues direct attention to the body ("bend your front knee," "high left elbow"). External cues direct attention to the effect or the environment ("drive the ball along the ground to that cone," "throw through the target"). A large body of research shows external cues generally produce better movement and better retention, because they let the body self-organise rather than freezing under self-monitoring. Default to external cues; reach for internal ones only when you must isolate a specific body part.

Feedback: less, later, and earned

The instinct is to comment on every ball. Resist it. Constant feedback makes players dependent on you and stops them developing their own feel for the game. Give feedback in smaller doses than feels natural, leave a gap so the player can self-assess first ("what did you feel there?"), and prioritise the one thing that will make the biggest difference over the five things you noticed.

  • Ask before you tell. "Where did that one go? Why?" builds a player who can coach themselves — the goal.
  • One point at a time. A beginner cannot process a checklist. Fix the biggest rock; the pebbles often sort themselves out.
  • Be specific, not generic. "Well done" teaches nothing. "Great — you watched that right onto the bat" tells them what to repeat.
Coach's Note · Praise the process

Praise effort, decisions and improvement — things the player controls — not raw outcomes or talent. "You backed your plan and played straight" builds a player who keeps trying hard things. "You're a natural" builds one who avoids challenges that might dent the label. This is the difference, in the research, between a growth and a fixed mindset — and you shape it with every comment.

Questioning

Good questions do more teaching than good answers. Open questions ("what options did you have there?") make players think; closed ones ("was that the right shot?") mostly check compliance. Give real thinking time after asking — count to five in your head before rescuing them. The goal across a season is to talk less and ask more, until the players are reading the game themselves.

Managing behaviour and the environment

Most misbehaviour in junior cricket is boredom or embarrassment in disguise. A well-paced session with everyone active prevents most of it before it starts. When you do need to manage behaviour: be calm and consistent, address the behaviour not the child, and never use exercise (laps, press-ups) as punishment — it teaches children that physical activity is a penalty, the opposite of what you want. Agree a few simple team standards with the players at the start of a season; rules they helped set are rules they help keep.

Chapter 5 · Part 1 FoundationsParents, the team & the wider group

You are coaching a community, not just eleven players.

Working with parents

Parents are the reason the child is there, the transport, the tea, and — handled badly — a coach's biggest headache. Handled well, they are your greatest asset. Set expectations early and in person: explain your philosophy (development over results at this age), what you need from them (support, punctuality, positive touchline behaviour), and how they can help without coaching from the boundary. Most difficult-parent situations trace back to expectations that were never set.

Coach's Note · The car-ride home

The most influential coaching moment of the week is one you're not present for: the drive home. Ask parents to make it about enjoyment, not analysis — "did you have fun?" rather than a debrief of every dismissal. Share this openly at your parents' meeting. It changes children's relationship with the game more than any drill you run.

Mixed abilities and stages in one group

You will rarely have a uniform group. The skill is to keep the same activity working for everyone by adjusting the challenge per player, not running five separate sessions. Constraints do this elegantly: the same target game can have a nearer cone for a beginner and a smaller gap for an advanced player. Pair or group by task so no one is left waiting or exposed, and rotate roles so every child bats, bowls, keeps and fields — early specialisation robs children of skills and of fun.

Inclusion — the default, not the add-on

Good junior cricket includes every child: girls and boys, disabled players, those from every background and body type, the naturally gifted and the willing trier. This is both right and practical — inclusive clubs are bigger, healthier clubs. Chapter 6 covers the safeguarding and formal inclusion frameworks in detail. The foundational habit to build now is simple: design so that the least confident child in your group has a good time and improves. If that child thrives, everyone above them does too.

Safety · Before you coach a single session

Make sure you are cleared to work with children under your board's or club's safeguarding policy, know who your club's welfare/safeguarding officer is, and have access to a first-aid kit and an emergency action plan for the venue. Part 2 explains each of these. None of the coaching craft in Part 1 matters if a child isn't safe.

Chapter 6 · Part 2 Safety & careSafeguarding & inclusion

The non-negotiable foundation. Get this right before anything else.

Coaching children is a privilege built on trust — from the child, the parents and the club. Safeguarding is how you honour that trust. It is not paperwork to be endured; it is the set of habits that keeps children safe and keeps you, as a coach, protected and above suspicion. Every board treats it as mandatory, and no coaching skill in this manual matters if a child is not safe.

Before you coach: the essentials

In India, the BCCI and its state associations require coaches working with minors to operate under child-protection norms; most established academies and clubs now require a background check, a signed code of conduct, and a named point of contact for welfare concerns. Wherever you coach, make sure the following are in place before your first session.

  • Clearance to work with children. A completed background/police verification as required by your club, academy or board.
  • A named safeguarding / welfare officer. Know who they are and how to reach them. Every child, parent and coach should know too.
  • A reporting route. You must know exactly what to do, and to whom, if a child discloses harm or you have a concern — before the moment arrives.
  • Consent and emergency details. Parental consent forms, medical information (asthma, allergies, conditions) and emergency contacts for every child.

Safe practice — the everyday habits

Most safeguarding is not about dramatic incidents; it is about routine professional boundaries that leave no room for harm or for doubt.

  • Avoid being alone with a single child. Work in open, observable spaces. If a one-to-one conversation is needed, stay in sight of others.
  • Follow the "rule of two" — aim for at least two adults present at sessions, especially with younger groups.
  • Physical contact only when necessary, and openly. If you must adjust a grip or demonstrate a movement, ask first, explain why, and do it in full view. Prefer showing and constraints over touching.
  • Careful communication. Contact players through parents, not privately; keep messaging on official group channels; never message a child individually.
  • Photography and social media only with consent and through club channels — never on personal accounts.
  • Appropriate changing and transport arrangements — coaches don't change with children, and travel is arranged transparently with parents.
Safety · If a child discloses harm

Listen calmly and take it seriously. Do not promise to keep it secret, do not investigate or question the child in detail, and do not confront anyone yourself. Reassure the child they were right to tell you, record what was said in their words as soon as possible, and pass it to your safeguarding officer without delay. Your job is to receive and report — not to judge or resolve.

Inclusion — cricket for every child

An inclusive session is a better session for everyone, and it is also how clubs grow. Design so that every child — regardless of gender, ability, background, body type or confidence — can take part, be challenged appropriately and enjoy it.

Girls in cricket

Girls' and women's cricket is the fastest-growing part of the game in India and worldwide. Make girls genuinely welcome: mixed groups at younger ages are fine and beneficial, ensure equal access to good coaching time and equipment, use inclusive language, and provide role models and appropriate facilities. Never water down the technical challenge for girls — coach them as cricketers.

Disability and para-cricket

Cricket has established formats for players who are blind or partially sighted, deaf, have a physical disability or a learning disability. At grassroots level, focus on adapting rather than excluding: modify the equipment (larger, softer, audible balls), the rules, the distances and the group size so that a disabled child plays alongside peers. The constraints-led approach from Chapter 3 is your best tool here — it is inclusion by design.

The STEP framework for adapting activities

A simple, reliable way to include mixed abilities in one activity is to vary any of four things: Space (make the area bigger or smaller), Task (change the rules or the goal), Equipment (softer ball, lighter bat, bigger target) and People (group sizes, roles, who is on which side). Change one letter to make an activity easier or harder for an individual without stopping the whole session.

Coach's Note · Design for the least confident

Set up every activity so that the least confident, least experienced child in your group can succeed and enjoy it — then add challenge for the others through STEP. If that child leaves wanting to come back, you have almost certainly served the whole group well.

Chapter 7 · Part 2 Safety & careFast-bowling workload

The single most important safety rule in youth cricket. Learn these numbers.

Fast bowling places extreme, repeated loads on a young, still-growing spine. Bowling too much, too young — especially during the growth spurt — is strongly linked to stress fractures of the lower back that can end careers before they begin. Every major board therefore publishes hard limits on how much a young fast bowler may bowl. Treat these as rules, not suggestions. Below are the widely used directives; the BCCI's junior guidelines follow the same principles and magnitudes, and your local association may set its own. Always apply the stricter of your competition's rules and these.

Match limits — overs per spell and per day

A "fast" or "medium-pace" bowler for these purposes is anyone the wicketkeeper would reasonably stand back for. The limits cap both a single continuous spell and the whole day.

Safety · The rest-between-spells rule

Once a young fast bowler completes a spell, they may not bowl again — from either end — until the same number of overs as their spell has been bowled from that end. A U14 who bowls a 5-over spell must wait for 5 overs to be bowled from that end before returning. This prevents a captain from working one bowler in repeated short bursts and quietly exceeding a safe daily load. (In T20 and shorter formats the between-spells wait is relaxed, but the per-day maximum still stands.)

Weekly load and recovery

Match limits are only half the picture — nets and training count too. The guiding principles:

  • Cap bowling days. In any 7-day period aim for a maximum of about 4 bowling days, with only one instance of bowling on consecutive days.
  • Rest scales with volume. A heavy bowling session (roughly 7–10+ overs) warrants two days' recovery before bowling again; a moderate session one day; a very light spell none.
  • Build in down-weeks and breaks. A lighter week every 4–5 weeks (around half the usual volume), a short mid-season break, and a genuine off-season break of several weeks with no bowling.

Indicative weekly bowling volume rises with age — roughly 12–16 overs a week for under-11s up to 24–28 for 18–19s — but the exact number matters less than the habit of monitoring it. Count overs across matches and nets, watch for a bowler who suddenly ramps up (a spike in load is the danger, not a steady high load), and keep a simple log.

Coach's Note · Watch for the warning signs

Any young fast bowler reporting lower-back pain, stiffness that lingers, or a change in their action to bowl "around" a niggle must stop bowling and be assessed — a back stress fracture often gives weeks of warning as mild ache before it becomes serious. Never coach a child to "run it off." When in doubt, rest and refer.

Mixed and developing actions

The other great cause of back injury is a mixed action — where the shoulders and hips rotate out of alignment at the moment of delivery, twisting the spine. Learning a well-aligned action early (side-on, front-on or midway, but consistent) is a safety issue as much as a performance one. Chapter 10 covers the technical detail; the safety point here is that the skill-hungry years (Chapter 2) are exactly when to invest in a sound, low-stress action rather than chasing raw pace.

Chapter 8 · Part 2 Safety & careInjury, equipment & the field

Preventing the avoidable, and being ready for the rest.

Protective equipment — what young players must wear

Hard-ball cricket demands proper protection, and for children some of it is non-negotiable.

Safety · Helmets are mandatory for young players

Players under 18 must wear a helmet with a faceguard/grille when batting against a hard ball, in matches and in practice. A young wicketkeeper standing up to the stumps must also wear a helmet with a faceguard or a keeper's face protector. Use a helmet that meets a recognised standard (e.g. BS 7928) and check the gap between peak and grille is too small for a ball to pass. Do not let a child bat or keep up without one, however short the session.

Beyond helmets, ensure correctly sized pads, gloves and an abdominal guard (box) for batters and wicketkeepers, and appropriate footwear. Equipment that is too big is both ineffective and a hazard — a bat too heavy distorts technique and tires a child fast. Size gear to the child, not the cupboard.

The field: protecting young fielders

Close fielding is dangerous for children whose reactions and size don't yet match a hard ball travelling fast. A firm regulation applies regardless of whether a helmet is worn:

Age groupMinimum fielding distance from the bat
Under 13 and youngerNot closer than 11 yards (10 m) from the middle stump until the batter has played at the ball
Under 15 and youngerNot closer than 8 yards (7.3 m) from the middle stump until the batter has played at the ball

Exception: behind the wicket on the off side is unrestricted. The distance applies even if the fielder wears a helmet. If a young fielder strays inside it, the umpire stops play and moves them back.

Warming up and preparing to play

A good warm-up prevents injury and primes performance. Use the RAMP structure: Raise the heart rate (light movement, a fun game of tag), Activate and Mobilise the muscles and joints used in cricket (lunges, arm circles, spinal rotations), and Potentiate with sharper, cricket-specific movements (build-up throws, run-throughs, shadow batting). Avoid long static stretches before play; save those for the cool-down. Bowlers in particular should build up their pace gradually — never bowl flat out on a cold arm.

Common youth cricket injuries

Most cricket injuries fall into a few families. Overuse injuries — chiefly the fast bowler's lower back (Chapter 7) — are the ones you prevent through load management. Impact injuries come from the ball: blows to the hand, head or body, prevented by proper equipment and fielding rules. Acute soft-tissue injuries — hamstring, groin, ankle — come from sprinting, turning and diving, reduced by good warm-ups and by not overloading tired children. For any blow to the head, apply the "if in doubt, sit them out" principle: suspected concussion means the child stops immediately and is not allowed to return that day, and is assessed by a medical professional before playing again.

Environment: heat, hydration and the ground

Cricket in India often means playing in significant heat. Ensure frequent water breaks (don't wait for children to say they're thirsty), shade for waiting players, sun protection and caps, and be willing to shorten or reschedule sessions in extreme heat. Before every session, walk the ground for hazards — holes, hard patches, glass, sightscreen and boundary safety, net condition — and know where your first-aid kit is and who is first-aid trained.

Safety · Have an Emergency Action Plan

Before your season, know for each venue: where the nearest phone and first-aid kit are, how an ambulance would access the ground, who is first-aid qualified, and where the emergency and parental-contact details are kept. Write it down. In an emergency you should be executing a plan, not improvising one.

Chapter 9 · Part 3 Technical skillsBatting

Watch the ball, be still, and let a balanced base do the work. Everything else is detail hung on those three ideas.

Batting is the discipline players most want to be coached in and the one most often coached badly — buried under a checklist of body parts until the child can no longer just watch the ball and hit it. Keep returning to the essentials: watch the ball closely, stay balanced, and play the line and length in front of you. The techniques below exist to serve those ends, not to replace them. Coach them through the game-based and constraints-led methods of Chapter 3, and fix one thing at a time (Chapter 4).

The grip — where control begins

Almost every technical fault downstream — a bat that comes down crooked, a face that won't stay closed, a batter who can't play straight — traces back to the grip. Get it right first and much else follows.

Both hands sit close together in the middle-to-top half of the handle. The key is alignment: if the batter makes a "V" with the thumb and forefinger of each hand, both V's should point down the back of the bat, between the outside (splice) edge and the middle — roughly toward the shoulder of the bat's back. The top hand (the left hand for a right-hander) is the control hand and grips firmly; the bottom hand is lighter, cradled more in the fingers, and provides guidance and power, not a stranglehold.

Stance and set-up

The stance is the platform. Feet about shoulder-width apart, either side of the crease, weight even on the balls of the feet, knees slightly flexed. The most important single element is the head: eyes level and still, both looking at the bowler, with the head over or just inside the front foot so weight is centred, not falling away. A batter whose head tilts or falls to the off side before the ball is bowled has already lost the line.

The bat rests behind the back foot or taps gently in the crease. The set-up should feel relaxed and repeatable — tension is the enemy of the fast, free movement batting needs. Let each player find a comfortable version of this; there is no single "correct" stance, only a balanced one with a still, level head.

Backlift and the downswing — playing straight

As the bowler runs in, the bat lifts back to prepare the strike. A backlift that goes straight back over the stumps, with the face pointing roughly down at the ground or gully, lets the bat come down straight through the line — the basis of every orthodox shot. The classic teaching image is the "V": for straight-bat drives, the bat swings within a V drawn from the batter down the ground between mid-on and mid-off, so the full face meets the ball and the follow-through goes toward the target.

Coach's Note · Don't over-coach the backlift

A high, textbook backlift is a means, not an end. Many great players have loops and quirks. What matters is that the bat arrives straight and on time at the ball. Rather than manipulating a child's backlift with instructions, use a target game — "drive that ball back past me along the ground" — and let a straight swing emerge from the task.

Reading length: the one decision that drives footwork

Everything a batter does with their feet is a response to length — where the ball pitches. Length dictates whether to go forward or back long before line dictates where to hit. This single reading skill separates competent batters from strokeless ones, and it is trainable from a young age with varied feeds (Chapter 3).

The shots, built in layers

Teach shots in an order that builds on the straight-bat foundation rather than starting with the glamorous ones. A sound sequence:

1 · Front-foot defence

The reference shot. Front foot to the pitch of the ball, head leading over the front knee, bat coming down straight and close to the front pad, soft hands to deaden the ball. Every forward drive is this shot with the brakes released.

2 · Back-foot defence

Against the shorter ball: back and across, weight over the back foot, playing the ball down under the eyes with a straight bat. The base of the back-foot game.

3 · The drives (off, straight, on, cover)

Front-foot defence extended into a full flowing swing through the V and beyond, hitting the ball along the ground. The cover drive goes squarer on the off side; the on-drive (hardest of all) works the straighter ball to the leg side — both demand the head staying still and over the ball.

4 · Back-foot square shots — the cut and pull

Cross-bat shots for width and height. The cut punishes the short, wide ball square on the off side, hitting down on it with the bat coming across. The pull and hook deal with the short ball into the body, swivelling to hit it to the leg side — high-risk against the rising ball and coached with care and helmets.

5 · The sweep family

Against spin: a controlled cross-bat shot to a fuller ball, front knee down, hitting with or against the turn. The conventional sweep, the paddle and later the reverse sweep expand a player's options against slow bowling — introduce once the orthodox base is secure.

Coach's Note · The order matters

A child taught to slog before they can defend learns to value the outcome (the ball sailing away) over the process (watching it, playing straight). Build defence and drives first; the attacking cross-bat shots layer on top far more soundly once the straight-bat swing is grooved. Power is easy to add later; a broken swing is hard to rebuild.

Running between the wickets

The most under-coached batting skill and the cheapest source of runs and wickets. Coach it as deliberately as stroke play: call early and loudly (three calls only — "yes," "no," "wait"), the striker calls for anything in front of square and the non-striker for anything behind, run the first run hard and turn looking for the second, ground the bat by sliding it in, and back up as the non-striker. Poor running runs out your best players and deflates a team; good running pressures the field and steals matches. Build it into small-sided games (Chapter 3) where singles are rewarded.

Power hitting — the modern layer

White-ball cricket has made clean hitting a genuine skill to coach, not a betrayal of technique. The foundation is the same still head and balanced base; power comes from bat speed through the ball, a full swing, and hitting with the momentum of the body — not from a bottom-hand heave. Coach it separately and later, once the orthodox base is sound, using clear-the-front-leg and hitting-zone constraints. It complements, rather than replaces, the ability to build an innings.

Chapter 10 turns to the other half of every ball — pace bowling — where the same principles of alignment, balance and repeatable movement reappear from the bowler's end.

Chapter 10 · Part 3 Technical skillsPace bowling

A repeatable, well-aligned action that protects the back and lands the ball on a length — pace and swing are what you add once that base is safe.

Fast bowling is the most physically demanding and injury-prone discipline in cricket (Chapter 7), which makes how you coach it a safety matter as much as a performance one. The order of priorities for a young quick is clear: first a safe, aligned, repeatable action; then accuracy — the ability to land the ball on a chosen length; then pace and movement. Coaches who chase raw speed first tend to build rushed, misaligned actions that break down. Build the machine before you rev the engine.

The action, phase by phase

A bowling action is one flowing movement, but it helps to coach it in five linked phases. A fault in an early phase almost always shows up as a problem in a later one, so diagnose backwards from the release to find the true cause.

Run-up

Smooth, rhythmic and repeatable, building gradually to a controlled maximum — not a flat sprint. Its only job is to deliver the bowler to the crease balanced and on the same spot every time. Length is individual: long enough to build momentum, short enough to stay controlled (younger bowlers need only a handful of strides). Mark it out and rehearse it so it never varies.

Gather and load ("the bound")

The moment the bowler jumps into the delivery, gathering their momentum, front arm reaching high, back foot landing to begin the delivery stride. This is where alignment is set — and where the mixed action (below) is born or avoided.

Delivery stride and the braced front leg

The front foot lands and the front leg braces — ideally straightening — to become a firm lever the body rotates over. A strong front-leg block is the single biggest source of both pace and safe force transfer. The front arm pulls down and in toward the front hip as the bowling arm comes over.

Release

The bowling arm passes close to the head at its highest point, the wrist behind the ball, snapping down through release. The head stays still and the eyes stay level on the target.

Follow-through

The bowler continues through and off the pitch, decelerating gradually over several strides. A cut-off follow-through both loses pace and jars the body; a full one protects it.

Action alignment — and the mixed-action danger

At the moment the back foot lands, the line of the hips and the line of the shoulders set the action's type. Three aligned types are all safe and effective; the dangerous case is the mixed action, where hips and shoulders point different ways and the lower spine is forced to twist violently. This is a leading cause of the back stress fractures discussed in Chapter 7 — so this diagram is a safety tool, not just a technical one.

Safety · Screen every young quick for a mixed action

Film the delivery from directly behind (bowler's-eye view) and from side-on. Look at back-foot landing: do the hips and shoulders point the same way? A visible split, a "falling away" to the off side, or a back-foot that lands then drags round are warning signs. Correcting alignment early — usually at the gather — prevents the injuries in Chapter 7. If you see a marked mixed action combined with back pain, stop bowling and refer.

Grip, seam and swing

The stock grip: first two fingers either side of the seam, thumb resting on the seam underneath, ball held in the fingers not jammed into the palm, seam upright. From this base come the two ways a new ball deviates.

Swing — through the air

Swing comes from presenting the seam at an angle while keeping one side of the ball smoother/shinier than the other. Angling the seam toward the slips with the shine on the leg side tends to produce the outswinger (moving away from a right-hander); reversing it produces the inswinger. The wrist stays firm behind the ball and the seam must stay steady in flight — a wobbling seam kills swing.

Seam movement — off the pitch

An upright, steady seam that lands on its stitches can deviate unpredictably off the surface. It requires no angle — just a firm wrist and a seam kept vertical — and is often more repeatable for young bowlers than swing. Bowling seam-up on a good length is the bread and butter of pace bowling.

Length, line and the channel

The most valuable skill after a safe action is landing the ball on a good length in the "corridor" just outside off stump — full enough to threaten the stumps and edge, short enough that the batter can't drive comfortably (revisit the length diagram in Chapter 9). Coach accuracy with target games: a length mat, cones for the channel, points for hitting the zone. Consistency of length beats variety at junior level — a bowler who repeatedly hits a good length is hard to score off and creates chances.

Variations — added last

Once action, accuracy and stock ball are secure, layer in variations sparingly: the slower ball (off-cutter, back-of-hand, or a grip change — disguised by the same action), the bouncer (used sparingly and legally against young players; see the short-ball safety note in Chapter 9), the yorker (a full, straight ball at the base of the stumps — a target-practice skill), and the cutter (fingers cutting across the seam for movement). Each is a change of pace or movement hung on an unchanged action, so the batter can't read it early.

Coach's Note · Rhythm over effort

Young bowlers equate "fast" with "strain," and tense up — which actually slows them down and risks the action falling apart. Cue smoothness and rhythm ("run in relaxed, let it flow"), not maximum effort. A relaxed, rhythmic action at 80% often produces more genuine pace, better accuracy and far less injury than an all-out heave. Pace is a by-product of rhythm and a good front-leg block, not of trying harder.

Chapter 11 turns to the other bowling craft — spin — where guile, flight and revolutions replace pace, and the risks to a young body are far lower.

Chapter 11 · Part 3 Technical skillsSpin bowling

Revolutions, flight and guile. The spinner's job is to deceive the batter in the air and off the pitch — and, unlike pace, it barely taxes a young body.

Spin bowling is the discipline most worth encouraging in young cricketers and most often neglected. It is far kinder to a growing body than pace (no braced-front-leg pounding, no stress-fracture risk), it rewards brain over brawn, and a good young spinner is precious because so few are properly coached. The core message to a beginner is counter-intuitive but vital: spin it hard first, control it second. A bowler who learns to really rip the ball can be taught accuracy later; one drilled only to land it develops no revolutions and never truly spins it.

The two families of spin

All spin bowling divides into two families by how the ball is spun, and each contains a stock ball that turns one way plus variations that go the other. For a right-handed bowler to a right-handed batter:

Grip and release — where the spin is made

Spin comes from the grip and the way the hand imparts revolutions at release, not from arm speed.

The off-break

Held across the seam with the first two fingers spread and gripping the seam; spin is imparted by rotating the wrist and rolling the index/middle fingers down and across the ball at release — the classic image is "turning a door handle" or opening a jar. The ball leaves over the top of the fingers and spins clockwise (from the bowler's view), gripping the pitch to turn from off to leg.

The leg-break

Held with the seam across the first two fingers and the third (ring) finger along the seam doing most of the work; the ball is spun by a vigorous flick of the wrist and third finger, coming out of the side/back of the hand and rotating the other way, so it turns from leg to off. It feels awkward at first and needs patient, playful practice — spinning a ball up in the air, spin-bowling at a wall — before any accuracy is expected.

The spinner's real weapons are in the air

Beginners think spin bowling is about turn off the pitch. In truth most wickets come from deception before the ball pitches. Coach these four qualities, which all flow from spinning the ball hard:

  • Flight: tossing the ball above the batter's eyeline so they misjudge its path and length. Inviting the drive, then beating it, is the spinner's craft.
  • Dip: heavy top-spin (revolutions with a forward tilt) makes the ball drop shorter than the batter expects — the ball "dips" and they are stranded reaching for it.
  • Drift: a well-spun ball drifts sideways in the air (often into the RH batter for an off-spinner, away for a leg-spinner) before it even turns — a double deception.
  • Turn & bounce: the deviation off the pitch itself, plus extra bounce from over-spin. The finishing move, but only after the air has done its work.
Coach's Note · Rip it, don't guide it

The most common mistake in junior spin coaching is prizing accuracy so early that the child learns to push the ball down the pitch with no revolutions. Let beginners spin it as hard as they can — even wildly — for weeks. Games like spinning the ball across a table, or seeing how much it fizzes, build the hand action. Accuracy is a control you add to a big spin; you cannot add spin to a grooved push.

Length, flight and the art of tempting

A spinner's length is different from a seamer's. Too short and it sits up to be cut and pulled; too full and it is a half-volley to drive. The prized length is the one that tempts the batter forward to drive but is just short of driveable — so flight and dip beat them in the air, or the turn beats the outside edge. Coach spinners to vary flight and pace subtly — a slower, higher ball, then a quicker, flatter one — using the same action, so the batter can't read length early. Bowling "wicket to wicket" with a hint of over-spin is the reliable base; the loop and the big turning ball are earned on top.

Variations — the mystery, added last

Every variation is only as good as the stock ball it disguises, and must come from an identical action so the batter can't detect it. Introduce these once the stock ball genuinely turns and lands:

  • Off-spinner's arm ball: held and released to go straight on (or drift away) with the angle, deceiving a batter playing for turn.
  • Off-spinner's top-spinner / undercutter: more over-spin for extra dip and bounce.
  • Leg-spinner's googly ("wrong'un"): released from the back of the hand so it turns the "wrong" way — from off to leg, the opposite of the leg-break. The classic mystery ball.
  • Leg-spinner's top-spinner and flipper: the top-spinner dips and goes straight with bounce; the flipper is squeezed out to skid on low and fast — an advanced, patience-demanding delivery.

Fields and tactics for spin

Spin bowling is a partnership with the field and the captain. Spinners buy wickets by tempting mistakes, so a spinner needs catchers in the right places (a slip or short third for the edge, a mid-off/mid-on to tempt the drive, a boundary rider for the mishit). Coach young spinners to bowl to a plan — set a batter up over an over with flight and pace changes rather than trying to take a wicket every ball — and to accept that being hit for the occasional boundary is part of the trade when tossing it up. Encourage bravery: a spinner who flattens their trajectory to avoid being hit stops being a spinner.

Chapter 12 leaves bowling for the discipline that touches every player on the field — fielding — where catching, ground work and throwing win more matches than any single bowler.

Chapter 12 · Part 3 Technical skillsFielding

The discipline that involves every player on every ball — and the cheapest place to win or lose a match. Catches win matches; runs saved are runs scored.

Fielding is the great leveller: it needs no special talent to become good at, it improves any team immediately, and yet it is the most neglected part of junior practice. It is also the most inclusive skill to coach — every child fields, so every child improves — and the easiest to make fun through games. Coach it with energy and standards, and treat it as seriously as batting and bowling. A team that catches well and stops the single applies relentless pressure; a team that drops and misfields hands the opposition a free innings.

Catching

Catching splits by the height and speed of the ball, each with its own technique, but all share three constants: watch the ball all the way into the hands, give with the catch to absorb the pace ("soft hands"), and get the body behind the line where possible.

The high catch

For a steepling ball: get under it early, feet set, and form a "cup" or "reverse cup" with fingers pointing up and little fingers together (for a ball above the head) or fingers down and thumbs together (for a ball at chest height). Watch it right into the fingers and give downward on impact to cushion it. Call loudly and early — "mine!" — to avoid collisions.

The flat, hard catch (slip / infield)

For a fast-travelling edge or drive: relaxed, low ready position, hands soft and out in front, eyes level. Watch the edge off the bat and let the hands give backward with the ball. Slip fielders watch the edge of the bat (or the keeper's gloves), stay low, and don't snatch.

The close catch

Reaction catching at short positions — hands ready, low, still, watching the bat. Remember the young-fielder distance rules from Chapter 8: children under 15 must not field closer than the mandated minimums regardless of a helmet.

Ground fielding and the retrieve

Three techniques cover almost all ground fielding, escalating with how urgently the ball must be returned.

  • The long barrier (defensive, safe): for a firmly hit ball when a run is unlikely — one knee down sideways beside the gathering hand, forming a wall so the ball can't get through, body behind the line. The technique to teach first, especially near the boundary where an overthrow is costly.
  • The attacking pick-up (walking/running in): fielders move in as the bowler bowls, attack the ball, and gather it beside the leading foot on the move, ready to throw in one motion. This is how you stop the single and threaten the run-out.
  • The one-handed pick-up and throw (run-out attempt): attacking a ball at pace, gathering one-handed by the boot and releasing in the same movement. An advanced skill, drilled once the basics are secure.
Coach's Note · Move in with the bowler

The single biggest team-fielding habit to instil: every fielder walks or jogs in as the bowler runs up, so they are moving toward the ball, balanced and switched on, at the moment it is hit. A static fielder is a flat-footed fielder. Make "in as he bowls" a non-negotiable team standard from the youngest age — it turns a passive field into an alert one.

Throwing and run-outs

A good throw is fast, flat and accurate to the top of the stumps (or to the keeper's gloves over the bails). Coach the mechanics: a strong side-on base, non-throwing arm pointing at the target, the throwing arm coming through high over the shoulder, and a follow-through toward the target with a transfer of weight from back foot to front. For longer distances teach the crow-hop (a small skip to build momentum) rather than a flat-footed heave. Prioritise hitting the target — a fast throw two feet wide beats a rocket that misses and runs away for overthrows.

Run-outs are a team skill: the thrower aims at one end, the receiver (keeper or fielder) takes it cleanly over the stumps and breaks the wicket, and everyone backs up. The backing-up habit — a fielder positioned behind the stumps at each end to stop overthrows — saves as many runs as the throw itself and must be drilled as part of every throwing practice.

Fielding positions — the map every coach must know

The names of fielding positions are cricket's shared language, and a young cricketer (and coach) should learn them early. The field is described relative to a right-handed batter, split into the off side (the side the bat faces) and the leg/on side (behind the batter's legs), and by distance — close catchers, the inner ring (saving the single), and the outfield (saving the boundary).

Fielding as a team, and fitness

Beyond individual technique, coach the collective habits that separate good fielding sides: constant communication and calling, backing up every throw at both ends, sprinting on singles to cut off twos, supporting the bowler with encouragement, and setting a tone of energy in the field. Good fielding is largely attitude, and attitude is coachable — praise effort, dives and backing-up as loudly as you praise catches. Fielding also carries much of a cricketer's athletic load (sprinting, diving, throwing), so the athletic-development work in Chapter 14 and the RAMP warm-up in Chapter 8 feed directly into it.

Chapter 13 · Part 3 Technical skillsWicketkeeping

The one player involved in every single ball. A great keeper is worth a bowler — and sets the whole field's energy — yet keeping is the least-coached specialism in junior cricket.

The wicketkeeper touches more balls than anyone, effects run-outs and stumpings, takes the edges that win matches, and — crucially at junior level — sets the tempo and noise of the whole fielding side. Because keeping looks difficult and needs specialist gloves, it often goes uncoached: the child who "doesn't mind" is stuck behind the stumps and left to fend for themselves. Resist that. Give your keepers real, regular coaching. The fundamentals are learnable and the rewards for the team are large. Above all, keeping is about watching the ball, patience, and soft, giving hands — the same watch-the-ball principle that runs through every discipline in this manual.

The stance

A comfortable, balanced crouch the keeper can hold ball after ball without tiring: feet about shoulder-width, weight on the balls of the feet, a full squat with the seat down and the back fairly straight, head still and eyes level. The gloves rest on the ground or just above it, fingers pointing down, relaxed and together. The keeper rises with the bounce of the ball, coming up from the crouch as the ball arrives rather than standing early. Rising too soon is the single most common keeping fault — it lifts the eyes and hands off the low ball.

Standing back vs. standing up

Where the keeper stands is dictated by the pace of the bowler, and the two positions are almost different skills.

Standing back — to the quicks

For fast and fast-medium bowling, the keeper stands deep enough that the ball arrives at a comfortable catching height (roughly waist-high) after one bounce or on the full. The job is catching: watch the ball from the bowler's hand, move the feet to get the body behind the line, and take it with soft hands giving back into the body. Take the ball beside or in front of the body, not at it. Diving takes to the leg and off side are a standing-back skill.

Standing up — to the spinners and slow-medium

For spin and slow bowling the keeper stands immediately behind the stumps. This is the harder art: the ball may turn, bounce or deviate late off the pitch, and the keeper must watch it all the way, take it late with the hands staying relaxed, and be ready to whip off the bails for a stumping the instant the batter overbalances. Patience is everything — the keeper must let the ball come and take it under the eyes, not snatch at it. Standing up well is a huge asset because it keeps batters in their crease, denying them the room to advance to spin.

Safety · Keepers standing up must wear a helmet or face protector

A young wicketkeeper standing up to the stumps must wear a helmet with a faceguard, or a wicketkeeper's face protector (Chapter 8). The ball can deflect off bat or pad from a metre away with no reaction time. This is non-negotiable, in matches and in practice alike, whatever the pace of the bowling being kept to.

Glove work — the give

The heart of keeping is soft, giving hands. As the ball arrives, the gloves "give" with it — recoiling toward the body to absorb the pace — rather than grabbing at it, which pops the ball out. Take the ball with both gloves together forming a cup, watch it right into the gloves, and let the hands travel with the ball. For the low ball, fingers point down and the gloves stay under the line; for the higher ball, fingers turn up. The keeper watches the ball out of the bowler's hand, momentarily onto the pitch, off the bat or past it, and all the way into the gloves — an unbroken watch that beginners lose halfway.

Footwork

Great keeping is quiet, efficient footwork. The keeper moves the whole body into line with the ball using small, quick steps, taking the ball in a balanced position rather than reaching with the hands from a static base. To the leg side especially (where the batter's body hides the ball late) the keeper must move quickly to get outside the line and take it cleanly. Coach footwork with underarm feeds and tennis balls first, building the habit of feet then hands before adding pace.

Stumpings and run-outs

The keeper is the team's sharpest wicket-taker off their own gather. For a stumping standing up: take the ball cleanly first, then bring the gloves back to break the stumps with the ball, all in one smooth motion — rushing the break before securing the ball is the classic error. For run-outs, the keeper receives the fielder's throw over the top of the stumps and breaks the wicket; coach taking the throw cleanly first, gloves up over the bails, and knowing which end the throw is coming to. The keeper also directs traffic — calling which end to throw to — because they can see the whole field and both batters.

Coach's Note · The keeper is your on-field energiser

A good junior keeper does more than catch: they chatter, encourage the bowler, back up throws, and lift the field's energy every ball. When picking and coaching keepers, value enthusiasm, concentration and resilience (they will miss some — everyone does) as much as raw glove skill. And rotate the role in younger age groups so several children learn it — never sentence one child to a season behind the stumps because no one else volunteered.

Chapter 14 · Part 4 Body & mindAthletic development

Build the athlete, not just the cricketer. Movement quality, appropriate loading, and protecting a growing body underpin every skill in Part 3.

Cricket rewards all-round athletes — runners, jumpers, throwers, twisters and sprinters — yet junior cricket training often ignores physical development entirely, or copies adult gym programmes that are wrong for growing bodies. The guiding rule, straight from the LTAD model of Chapter 2, is to match the physical work to the developmental stage: build broad movement skills early, add the trainable qualities as the body becomes ready for them, and always load conservatively around growth.

Physical literacy first

Before sport-specific fitness comes physical literacy — the fundamental movement skills that make a competent mover: running, jumping and landing, throwing and catching, balancing, agility, coordination, twisting and bracing. For under-12s this is the physical programme, and it is best delivered through varied games and play rather than drills. A child who can move well learns cricket skills faster and gets injured less. Encourage multi-sport participation at young ages for exactly this reason — early single-sport specialisation narrows movement and raises overuse-injury risk.

What is trainable, and when

Different physical qualities respond best to training at different stages, loosely tied to the growth spurt (peak height velocity). These are windows of emphasis, not hard gates — everything is trainable to some degree at any age — but they tell you where to put the focus.

Safety · Strength training and growing bodies

Young children should not do heavy loaded weight training. Bodyweight movement — squatting, lunging, hopping, throwing, climbing, medicine-ball work — builds strength safely and is plenty until after the growth spurt. Formal resistance training with meaningful load should wait until skeletal maturity is well underway and must be taught with correct technique under qualified supervision. During rapid growth, be extra cautious with all loading: growth plates and tendon attachments are vulnerable.

Warming up and cooling down — RAMP

Every session and match starts with a structured warm-up. The RAMP model (introduced in Chapter 8) is the reliable template — and, done as a cricket activity, it doubles as skill practice.

Managing load and recovery

The most important physical-management job in youth cricket is watching total load — and for fast bowlers, this is a formal safety duty (Chapter 7). But it applies to every player: cricket seasons are long, children often play for school and club and academy at once, and it is easy for a keen young player to accumulate a dangerous total. Watch for spikes (sudden jumps in training or playing volume are the danger, more than a steady high load), ensure genuine rest days and an off-season break, and coordinate with a child's other coaches so no one is loading them blind. Sleep, nutrition and hydration matter as much as any drill — a growing, well-rested, well-fed child adapts and improves; a chronically tired one breaks down.

Coach's Note · Fitness through cricket, not laps

You rarely need to run separate "fitness" sessions for juniors. A well-designed, high-tempo practice full of small-sided games, fielding and running between wickets builds all the fitness young players need — and keeps it fun. Punishment laps and drills teach children that fitness is a chore; games teach them it's the price of admission to more play.

Chapter 15 · Part 4 Body & mindMental skills

Cricket is a game of failure, waiting and pressure. The mental side is coachable — and at junior level, largely about building a healthy relationship with mistakes.

Cricket is psychologically unique among team sports: a batter's error is final and public (one mistake and you're out), bowlers and fielders must sustain concentration through long quiet spells, and the game is riddled with waiting. Even great players fail far more often than they succeed. For young players this can be brutal, and how you coach the mental side shapes whether they thrive or quit. The foundation is not sports-psychology jargon — it is an environment where mistakes are treated as information, effort is valued over outcome, and children learn simple tools to focus and recover.

A healthy relationship with failure

The most valuable mental skill you can build in a young cricketer is resilience in the face of the game's constant failure. This starts with the environment you create (Part 1): praise process over outcome (Chapter 4), treat a dismissal or a bad over as a chance to learn rather than a disaster, and never let a child's mistakes be met with visible frustration from the coach or touchline. A batter who fears getting out plays tentatively and learns nothing; one who is free to fail plays with freedom and improves. Teach the reframe explicitly: "what did that ball teach you?" beats "how could you get out like that?"

Concentration and the pre-ball routine

Concentration is not holding intense focus for hours — that's impossible. It is learning to switch focus on and off: relax between balls, then switch fully on as the bowler runs in. The tool that builds this is the pre-ball routine — a short, repeatable sequence a player runs before every ball to arrive focused and calm. It is the same principle for batter, bowler and fielder.

Simple tools that work with juniors

  • Breathing to reset. One slow breath between balls, or after a mistake, settles the body and clears the head. The simplest, most reliable tool for a nervous young player.
  • A focus cue. One or two words that direct attention outward ("watch the ball," "hit the top of off") — external cues, as in Chapter 4 — rather than a checklist of technique that causes freezing.
  • Process goals. Set goals the player controls (watch every ball closely, back your shot, hit your length) rather than outcome goals (score 30, take a wicket). Controllable goals reduce anxiety and, ironically, improve outcomes.
  • Positive, specific self-talk. Coach children to notice and reframe harsh self-talk. Cricket's failures make negative self-talk common; a simple, kind, task-focused phrase is a learnable replacement.
  • Routines for the wait. Give fielders and waiting batters a way to switch off and back on, so long spells don't drain them.
Coach's Note · You are the mental environment

At junior level, formal mental-skills drills matter far less than the emotional climate you set. A calm coach who treats failure as normal, celebrates brave attempts, and never rides a child for mistakes teaches resilience more powerfully than any exercise. Watch your own touchline body language — children read it instantly, and it becomes their inner voice.

The wellbeing guidance in this manual's foundations applies here too: stay alert to signs a child is struggling beyond ordinary sporting disappointment, keep sport a source of joy and confidence rather than pressure, and involve parents and, where needed, appropriate support if a young player's distress runs deeper than the game.

Chapter 16 · Part 4 Body & mindTactics & captaincy

Where the individual skills meet the match. Teach players to read the game and make their own decisions — the coach's job is to develop thinkers, not to run the team by remote control.

Tactics tie the technical skills of Part 3 into a contest. At junior level the aim is not to impose a professional playbook but to help young players understand the game and make good decisions themselves — which is also the whole point of the game-based coaching in Chapter 3. A cricketer who can read a situation, weigh options and choose is worth more than one drilled to follow instructions. Coach the why behind decisions and hand ownership to the players.

Reading the game — the variables

Every tactical decision weighs a handful of variables: the match situation (score, wickets, overs left, required rate), the conditions (pitch, weather, boundary sizes, new or old ball), the batter (their strengths, weaknesses and current confidence), and the bowler's resources. Teach players to notice these and ask "what does this situation demand?" — building batters who pace an innings, bowlers who bowl to a plan, and fielders who anticipate.

Field settings — attack vs. defend

A field is a statement of intent, and its shape follows the plan. An attacking field brings fielders up to catch and pressure — used with the new ball, against a new batter, or when chasing wickets — accepting that gaps may leak runs. A defensive field spreads fielders to the boundary to stop runs — used to contain, protect a total, or dry up a set batter — accepting fewer catching chances. The captain and bowler choose a field that matches the plan for that batter and that moment.

Batting tactics — building and pacing an innings

Teach batters to bat in phases and to their situation: assess conditions early and value their wicket when new, build through rotating the strike (the running skills of Chapter 9 are a tactic as much as a technique), target the weaker bowlers and the gaps the field leaves, and accelerate late or when set. In a chase, break the target into manageable pieces and keep the required rate in view. The core lesson: good batting is decision-making — choosing the right ball to attack and the right ball to respect — not just stroke play.

Bowling tactics — bowling to a plan

A thinking bowler sets a batter up rather than hoping for a wicket every ball: build pressure with consistent line and length (the corridor of Chapter 10, the tempting length of Chapter 11), attack a batter's known weakness, use the field and variations with intent, and change pace or angle to break a partnership. Pressure from dot balls creates the wicket-taking mistake — so containment and attack work together, not in opposition.

Formats and how they change the game

Young cricketers meet several formats, and each rewards different tactics. Short white-ball games (T20 and the junior formats below) put a premium on power hitting, boundary-hitting, tight death bowling and athletic fielding; longer one-day formats reward building innings and sustained bowling pressure; multi-day and red-ball cricket, met later, rewards patience, technique and wearing the opposition down. Coach players to adapt their decisions to the format rather than playing one way always.

Junior formats — the modified game

Age-group cricket is deliberately modified so the contest fits the child: smaller pitches and boundaries, lighter and softer balls, fewer overs, and rules that keep everyone involved — batting pairs who don't leave on getting out, everyone bowling, retirement at a set score so all get a bat. In India, entry formats and school/club age-group competitions follow these principles, with the BCCI and state associations running the U14/U16/U19 age-group structures above. Coach within the format's spirit: at the youngest ages, maximum participation and fun; competitive intensity is layered on as players move up (the LTAD progression of Chapter 2).

Captaincy — a skill to teach, not assume

Captaincy is a genuine skill and a wonderful developmental tool, so coach it and rotate it. A junior captain learns to set fields, manage bowlers (within the fast-bowling limits of Chapter 7), read the game, support teammates and uphold the Spirit of Cricket (Chapter 1). Give young captains real decisions and the freedom to get them wrong, debrief with questions rather than criticism, and share the role around so many children develop leadership rather than one child owning it. A team of thinkers, led by a rotating captain who is learning, is the junior ideal.

Coach's Note · Coach decisions, not just outcomes

When reviewing a match, praise good decisions even when they didn't come off, and question poor decisions that happened to work. "That was the right field for the plan — well thought out" teaches more than "you got a wicket, great." Develop players who can captain themselves, and you have done the deepest tactical coaching there is.

Chapter 17 · Part 5 PlanningSession design

A great session is designed, not improvised. Every minute has a purpose, every player is active, and the whole thing points at one or two clear outcomes.

The difference between a session that develops players and one that merely fills time is planning. This does not mean a rigid script — the best coaches adapt constantly — but it does mean arriving with a clear intention, a shape, and activities designed to deliver it. Everything in the earlier parts of this manual comes together here: you deliver technical skills (Part 3) through the methods of Part 1, inside the safety framework of Part 2, developing the whole athlete of Part 4. This chapter is how you package that into ninety minutes on a Tuesday.

Start with the outcome

Plan backwards from a clear, narrow objective: "by the end, players can run the ball into the gaps and call early," not "we'll do some batting." One or two outcomes per session is plenty — a session that tries to cover everything develops nothing. Pick the outcome from where your players actually are (Part 7's assessment feeds this), make it specific and observable, and let it drive every activity you choose.

The shape of a session — whole–part–whole

The most effective and engaging session shape starts and ends with the game, with focused development in the middle. This whole–part–whole structure means players meet the skill in its real context first (so they see why it matters), work on the specific piece, then return to the game to apply it. It beats the old "drill for an hour, play for ten minutes" model on both engagement and transfer (Chapter 3).

Keeping everyone active — station and small-sided design

The enemy of a good junior session is the queue. A child standing in line is not learning and is very likely misbehaving (Chapter 4). Design for maximum activity: run stations (small groups rotating through several activities so no one waits), use small-sided games (2v2, 3v3 so everyone is involved every ball), and pair players to feed and work each other. Aim for the highest ratio of ball-contacts-per-child you can manage. If you have helpers or parents, brief them to run stations so groups stay small.

Differentiating within one activity

Your group will span abilities and stages. Rather than running separate sessions, adjust the challenge within the same activity using the STEP framework from Chapter 6 (Space, Task, Equipment, People) — a nearer target for one child, a smaller gap for another, a softer ball, a harder rule. Well-designed activities let every child work at the edge of their ability at the same time (the "just hard enough" success rate from Chapter 3).

Coach's Note · Plan it, then read the room

Arrive with a plan — and hold it loosely. If an activity is flying, let it run; if it's flopping, change it without ceremony. The plan's job is to make sure you thought hard beforehand and have a purpose to return to, not to force a session that isn't working. The best coaches plan thoroughly and then coach the players in front of them.

Indoor, small-space and large-group sessions

Reality rarely gives you a perfect ground. Indoors or in small spaces, use softer balls, shorter distances, and games that fit the area; the constraints often improve learning (tighter targets, quicker reactions). For large groups with few helpers, lean hard on stations and small-sided games, set clear routines and signals to manage transitions, and keep instructions brief and visual. Whatever the constraints, the principles hold: everyone active, clear outcomes, game-based, safe.

Chapter 18 · Part 5 PlanningSeason & group planning

Zoom out from the single session to the arc of a season and a year — so development is progressive and deliberate, not a random walk of whatever came to mind that week.

A series of good sessions is not the same as a good programme. Without a plan across the season, coaching drifts — the same favourite drills recur, some skills are never touched, and there is no sense of building. Planning at the season and annual scale makes development progressive: skills are introduced, developed and consolidated in a sensible order, physical load is managed across a long calendar, and the programme peaks and rests at the right times.

The annual cycle — periodisation, simply

Periodisation just means dividing the year into phases with different emphases, so training builds toward the season and the body gets planned recovery. For junior cricket it can be simple: a preparation phase, the competitive season, and an off-season break. The balance of skill work, physical work, practice and matches shifts across these phases.

Fit this to your reality: school and club seasons, exam periods, and the Indian calendar's heat and monsoon all shape when phases fall. The key habits are to build a fitness and technique base before the matches start, maintain and sharpen (not overhaul) skills during the season while managing load, and protect a real off-season break — especially the post-season bowling rest that prevents the injuries of Chapter 7.

Planning a block of sessions

Between the single session and the whole year sits the block — a handful of weeks (say a half-term) built around a theme or a small set of outcomes. Sketch a block so sessions build on each other: introduce a skill, develop it across a few sessions, then consolidate it in match-like play, before moving to the next theme. Revisit earlier skills so they aren't forgotten (the varied, spaced practice of Chapter 3 applies across weeks, not just within a session). A term plan can be a single page: weeks down the side, themes and outcomes across.

Balancing the programme across a squad

Over a season, make sure every player gets a genuine all-round development: everyone bats, bowls (pace and spin), keeps and fields, rather than being pigeonholed early (Chapter 2's warning on specialisation). Track roughly what each child has worked on so gaps don't open up — the quiet, compliant child can easily be under-served while the loud or gifted one dominates your attention. Plan deliberately for equity of coaching time and opportunity.

Match management within the plan

Matches are part of the development plan, not separate from it. At junior level, plan for participation: everyone bats and bowls, batting orders and bowling rotations are shared out across the season, and positions are rotated so children experience different roles. Enforce the fast-bowling limits (Chapter 7) as a planning constraint, not an afterthought. Use the post-match review to feed the next block — what did the matches reveal that sessions should address?

Coach's Note · Write it down, keep it light

A season plan does not need to be a thick document. A one-page annual sketch (phases and breaks), a one-page term/block plan (themes and outcomes), and your per-session template (Chapter 17) are enough to transform coaching from week-to-week improvisation into deliberate development. Plan on a page; adapt in the moment.

Warm-ups & movement games 10 drills

Use these to Raise, Activate and Mobilise (the RAMP model, Chapter 14) while sneaking in movement skills and fun. Keep them busy, loud and short — they set the tone for the whole session.

Drill 01

Cricket Tag

U8–U14whole groupcones, bibs5–8 min
Set up

Mark a square with cones (about 15×15 m for ~12 kids). Pick 2–3 taggers wearing bibs.

Run it
  1. On "go," taggers try to tag the others by hand inside the square.
  2. A tagged player does a quick cricket action to rejoin — 3 shadow drives, or 5 fast feet — then plays on.
  3. Swap taggers every 60–90 seconds so everyone works.
Coach

Heads up, change of direction, using space. This is a "Raise" activity — you want them warm and smiling, not queuing.

Easier: bigger square, more taggers. Harder: smaller square, taggers hold a ball and must touch with it.
Drill 02

Ball-Handling Circle

U8–U16groups of 6–81 ball per group4–6 min
Set up

Players stand in a circle, arm's length apart.

Run it
  1. Start by passing a soft ball hand-to-hand around the circle, both directions.
  2. Progress to underarm lobs across the circle, calling a name before throwing.
  3. Add a second and third ball so several are moving at once.
  4. Finish with quick, flat underarm feeds to build reaction catching.
Coach

Watch the ball into the hands, soft "give," call early. A gentle "Activate" for hands and eyes.

Easier: one ball, big soft ball, closer circle. Harder: more balls, smaller/quicker ball, step in to shrink the circle.
Drill 03

Dynamic Mobility Line

U10–U19whole groupcones5 min
Set up

Players spread on a start line; cones 20 m away.

Run it
  1. They move to the cones and back using a called movement each time: high knees, heel flicks, lunges with a twist, side shuffles, leg swings, arm circles.
  2. Build from gentle to sharper (walking lunges → skipping → strides).
  3. Finish with two build-up run-throughs at ~75% pace.
Coach

Full, controlled ranges of motion — this "Mobilises" the joints cricket uses. Keep it flowing, not static.

Easier: shorter distance, fewer movements. Harder: add a ball to carry/handle, combine two movements per length.
Drill 04

Numbers Reaction Game

U8–U16whole groupcones5 min
Set up

Number four cones 1–4 around a small area.

Run it
  1. Players jog on the spot in the middle.
  2. Coach calls a number; everyone sprints to that cone and back.
  3. Add rules: "odd = backpedal," "even = side shuffle," to sharpen decision-making.
Coach

Acceleration, quick first step, listening under mild pressure — a link to the concentration switch of Chapter 15.

Easier: fewer cones, slower calls. Harder: call two numbers in sequence; add a catch on return.
Drill 05

Partner Mirror Footwork

U10–U19pairsnone4 min
Set up

Pairs face each other about 2 m apart in a small box of cones.

Run it
  1. One player leads with quick side-to-side and forward-back movements.
  2. The partner mirrors as closely as possible.
  3. Swap leader every 30 seconds.
Coach

Light, balanced feet on the balls of the feet — the footwork base for batting, keeping and fielding.

Easier: slower lead, bigger box. Harder: add a ball toss at random for the follower to catch.
Drill 06

Shadow Skills Round

U8–U16whole groupbats optional4 min
Set up

Players spread out with space to swing safely.

Run it
  1. On calls, players shadow cricket movements: forward defence, cover drive, bowling action, keeping take, throw.
  2. Do 3–5 slow reps of each, then a couple at game speed.
Coach

Grooves movement patterns with no ball pressure — a "Potentiate" step that rehearses today's skill focus.

Easier: slower, fewer patterns. Harder: link a sequence (backlift → stride → drive) in flow.
Drill 07

Rob the Nest

U8–U12whole groupballs, hoops/cones6 min
Set up

A pile of balls in a central hoop; teams each have an empty hoop in a corner.

Run it
  1. On "go," one runner per team sprints to collect a ball and returns it to their hoop.
  2. Next teammate goes when the runner is back (relay).
  3. When the centre empties, teams may "rob" from other nests. Most balls after 2 minutes wins.
Coach

Pure fun and conditioning — sprinting, turning, energy. Great early-session "Raise."

Easier: no robbing, shorter runs. Harder: carry the ball in a specific grip; longer distance.
Drill 08

Catch-and-Move Grid

U10–U19groups of 41 ball5 min
Set up

Four players at four cones forming a square about 8 m across.

Run it
  1. Player throws underarm/overarm to the next cone, then follows their throw to that cone.
  2. Everyone is constantly moving around the square behind the ball.
  3. Reverse direction on a call.
Coach

Move feet to the ball, catch beside the body, throw accurately — a link into the fielding chapter.

Easier: underarm only, bigger square. Harder: flat fast throws; two balls going opposite ways.
Drill 09

Traffic Lights

U8–U12whole groupnone4 min
Set up

Open space; players jogging around.

Run it
  1. "Green" = jog, "amber" = high knees on the spot, "red" = freeze in a balanced athletic stance.
  2. Add cricket calls: "catch!" (jump and take a high catch mime), "dive!" (safe ground dive on grass).
Coach

Listening, balance, and a gentle rise in heart rate. Good for the youngest groups.

Easier: fewer commands. Harder: quick-fire opposite rules (green = freeze) to test focus.
Drill 10

Throw-Down Slip Warm-Up

U12–U19groups of 3–41 ball5 min
Set up

One feeder with a ball; 2–3 catchers in a low slip stance a few metres away.

Run it
  1. Feeder throws the ball down just in front so it comes up to catching height at gentle pace.
  2. Catchers take it with soft hands and roll it back.
  3. Vary height and side; rotate the feeder.
Coach

Low, still, relaxed hands; watch it in — primes the slip-catching skills of Chapter 12.

Easier: slower, higher bounce, tennis ball. Harder: faster, wider, closer catchers (mind fielding-distance rules for under-15s).

Batting drills 12 drills

These build the batting method of Chapter 9 in order: grip and swing first, then watching the ball, reading length, and finally placement and running under game pressure. Helmets on against a hard ball, always.

Drill 11

Grip & Mirror Check

U8–U16individual/pairsbat4 min
Set up

Each player with a correctly sized bat, space to hold it up.

Run it
  1. Player picks the bat off the ground naturally (this usually finds a decent grip).
  2. Check the two Vs (thumb–forefinger of each hand) point down the back edge, hands together (Chapter 9).
  3. Lift into a backlift and freeze; partner or coach checks the face points down/at gully.
  4. Repeat, releasing the bottom-hand grip to "light."
Coach

V-alignment, hands together, light bottom hand. Fix this before anything else.

Easier: coach places hands. Harder: eyes closed, then check; repeat until it's automatic.
Drill 12

Drop-Ball Drive

U8–U14pairsbat, soft balls8 min
Set up

Batter in stance; feeder stands beside/in front holding a ball out at waist height.

Run it
  1. Feeder simply drops the ball; batter steps forward and drives it along the ground after one bounce.
  2. Start stationary, then let the batter take a small forward stride to the bounce.
  3. 10 reps, then swap.
Coach

Head over the ball, straight bat down the line, hit it along the ground. Removes bowling variables so the swing is the only focus.

Easier: tee the ball on a cone (no drop). Harder: feeder drops to varying spots so the batter must move to it.
Drill 13

Tee / Cone Hitting

U8–U16individual/pairsbatting tee, bat, balls8 min
Set up

Ball on a batting tee (or cone) at the right height for the shot being practised.

Run it
  1. Set the tee full and straight for the drive; the batter strides and hits down the ground.
  2. Move the tee wider for the cover drive, shorter/higher for the cut.
  3. Groove 8–10 of one shot, then change the tee position.
Coach

Full swing, still head, balanced finish. A pressure-free way to build a repeatable shot shape.

Easier: bigger/softer ball, lower tee. Harder: call the shot only as they set up, forcing quick set-up.
Drill 14

Hit the V (Target Drive)

U10–U19pairs/small groupcones, bat, balls10 min
Set up

Place two cones down the ground forming a "gate" (the V of Chapter 9), 5–8 m in front of the batter.

Run it
  1. Feeder underarms/throws a full ball; batter drives to hit it through the gate along the ground.
  2. Score a point for each ball through the gate on the floor.
  3. Sets of 6; batters compete for the highest score.
Coach

External target does the coaching — a straight swing emerges. Reward along-the-ground placement.

Easier: wider gate, closer, softer feed. Harder: narrow the gate; add a second gate for the cover drive.
Drill 15

Colour / Number Ball (Watch the Ball)

U10–U19pairsmarked balls, bat8 min
Set up

Balls marked with 2–3 colours or big numbers. Batter in a net or safe zone.

Run it
  1. Feeder bowls/throws; batter must call the colour or number before playing the shot.
  2. Only count the shot if the call is correct.
  3. Progress from underarm to a proper bowled feed.
Coach

Forces genuine ball-watching right out of the hand — the root skill of batting (Chapter 15's focus cue in action).

Drill 16

Length Reaction (Forward or Back)

U12–U19pairs/netballs, bat, helmet10 min
Set up

Mark a full zone and a short zone on the pitch with chalk/cones. Batter with helmet.

Run it
  1. Feeder randomly pitches into the full or short zone.
  2. Batter reads it and moves: forward to the full ball, back to the short.
  3. No pre-movement allowed — "watch it pitch, then go."
Coach

Reading length and committing late (Chapter 9). This is the decision that unlocks footwork.

Easier: tell them the length first, then remove that cue. Harder: add a good-length zone that must be defended solidly.
Drill 17

Gate Defence (Bat–Pad Gap)

U12–U19pairs/netballs, bat, helmet8 min
Set up

Place a cone just outside the batter's front pad as a "gate" not to leave open.

Run it
  1. Feeder bowls a good-length straight ball.
  2. Batter defends forward with bat next to pad, closing the gate.
  3. Coach checks no gap between bat and pad at contact.

Chapter 20 · Part 7 Assessment & developmentPlayer assessment

You can't develop what you don't measure — but at junior level, measure to help the child improve, never to rank or label them.

Assessment closes the coaching loop: it tells you where each player is, so your session outcomes (Chapter 17) and season plans (Chapter 18) target real needs rather than guesses. Done well, it is a development tool the player owns. Done badly — as public ranking, early labelling, or a hunt for "talent" — it damages confidence and drives children out of the game. The whole spirit of this manual (Part 1) applies: assess the process and the improvement, not just the outcome, and keep it kind, specific and forward-looking.

What to assess

Look across the whole cricketer, not just whether they scored runs. A rounded picture covers the technical (the skills of Part 3), the tactical (decisions and game understanding, Chapter 16), the physical (movement, athleticism, Chapter 14), and the personal/mental (attitude, resilience, coachability, teamwork, Chapter 15). A child weak in one area and strong in another is the norm — the point is to see the full shape so you can help.

How to assess — mostly by watching

At junior level your best tool is your own informed observation during games and drills, guided by the technique and fault-diagnosis chapters. Formal methods have their place but keep them light:

  • Observation against simple criteria. Watch a specific skill in a game context and note what you see against the coaching points from Part 3.
  • Skill checklists / benchmarks. A short list of "can do" markers per age band (below) — a developmental map, not a pass/fail test.
  • Video. Filming an action (batting, bowling) and reviewing it with the player is powerful and objective — and, for bowling alignment, a safety check (Chapter 10).
  • Self- and peer-assessment. Ask players to judge their own performance ("what went well, what next?") — it builds the self-coaching habit of Chapter 4.

The most useful output of assessment is a simple, collaborative plan for each player. An IDP need not be elaborate — a few lines, agreed with the child (and shared with parents), turning assessment into action:

  • One or two priorities to work on next (not a long list — Chapter 4's "one thing at a time").

  • A review point to revisit and update it.
Coach's Note · Assess to encourage, not to sort

Frame every assessment around growth: "here's what you do well, here's the next thing, here's how." Avoid comparing children to each other, avoid early labels ("he's our fast bowler," "she can't bat") that become self-fulfilling, and remember the relative-age and late-developer traps of Chapter 2 — the child who looks ordinary at 12 may be your best player at 17 if you keep developing, not sorting.

Chapter 21 · Part 7 Assessment & developmentThe pathway & your development as a coach

Where players can go, how to support them without pushing too soon — and how to keep getting better yourself.

The player pathway in India

Understanding the pathway helps you guide players and parents realistically. In India, cricket runs from school and club cricket up through district and state age-group structures to the national level, with the BCCI and its state associations administering age-group tournaments (commonly U14, U16/U15, U19 and U23) and the National Cricket Academy (NCA) supporting elite development. Talented juniors are typically spotted through school and club cricket, selected into district and then state age-group squads, and may progress to zonal, national age-group and eventually first-class (Ranji Trophy) and international cricket.

Coach's Note · Support ambition, resist over-pushing

When a child shows real ability and drive, help them find the next opportunity (a good academy, a district trial) and guide parents honestly. But guard against the classic junior-cricket harms: early specialisation, excessive load (especially bowling — Chapter 7), and turning a child's game into an adult's project. Keep it the child's journey, keep it enjoyable, and let ambition grow from the player, not be imposed on them.

Coaching qualifications and continued learning

Formal courses sharpen your craft and are increasingly expected. In India the BCCI runs a coach-education pathway — an entry-level module (Level 0) as a prerequisite, then Level 1, Level 2 and Level 3 (advanced) certifications through the NCA — and international bodies (ECB, Cricket Australia) run parallel level-based schemes. A course is a milestone, not the finish line: the best coaches keep learning continuously from other coaches, reading and watching, reflecting on their own sessions, and seeking feedback from players and peers.

The reflective coach — revisited

Chapter 1 introduced the habit of reflection; here it becomes your engine of improvement. Keep it simple and consistent: after each session note what you intended, what actually happened for the players, and one thing to change. Over a season this record — more than any single course — is what makes you better. Ask yourself the honest questions: Were all my players active and improving? Did I talk too much? Did the quiet child get as much of me as the loud one? Did I coach decisions, or just outcomes?

Coach's Note · You are a developing athlete too

Treat your own coaching the way you treat your players' cricket: expect to be imperfect, value the process over any single session's outcome, seek feedback without ego, and keep working on one thing at a time. A coach who visibly keeps learning models exactly the growth mindset (Chapter 4) they want their players to have.

The whole manual, in one idea

Every part of this manual serves a single aim, the one from the very first page: to develop young cricketers who still love the game, still want to train, and have the skills and movement to keep improving — for years, not weekends. Coach the child in front of you, keep them safe, make it fun, build sound skills through games, and keep learning yourself. Do that, and whether or not any one player reaches the top of the pathway, you will have done the most important job in cricket.

C · Pre-session safety & risk checklist

Run through before every session (Chapters 6–8).

  • Ground walked and hazards cleared (holes, glass, hard patches); nets/sightscreens/boundaries safe.
  • First-aid kit present; first-aider identified; Emergency Action Plan known for this venue.
  • Helmets available and worn for batting and keeping up to the stumps; correct-sized protective kit.
  • Fast-bowling loads planned within age limits; young-fielder distances (11 yd U13 / 8 yd U15) respected.
  • Register taken; medical/consent info and emergency contacts to hand; adequate adult supervision (rule of two).
  • Water and shade available; session adjusted for heat if needed.

E · Glossary of key terms

Backlift — the raising of the bat in preparation to play a shot. Constraints-led — coaching by changing the task/environment so better movement emerges. Corridor — the channel just outside off stump where seamers aim. Doosra — an off-spinner's ball that turns the "wrong" way. Drift — sideways movement of a spun ball in the air. Full/good/short length — where the ball pitches, dictating the batter's footwork. Googly / wrong'un — a leg-spinner's ball that turns from off to leg. LTAD — Long-Term Athlete Development, the stage model of Chapter 2. Mixed action — a dangerous bowling action with hips and shoulders misaligned. Off/leg side — the two halves of the field, defined by the batter's stance. RAMP — Raise, Activate, Mobilise, Potentiate warm-up structure. Seam/swing — deviation off the pitch / through the air. Spirit of Cricket — the code of fair play in the Preamble to the Laws. STEP — Space, Task, Equipment, People — the framework for adapting activities. TGfU — Teaching Games for Understanding, the game-based method of Chapter 3. Whole–part–whole — the session shape of Chapter 17.
